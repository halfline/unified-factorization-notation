Already know arithmetic? A short introduction to UFN.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic says that every positive whole number greater than one has a unique factorization into primes, apart from their order. UFN makes that factorization the number's spelling: each position names a prime, and its entry gives the exponent, written in UFN too.

Decimal places describe powers of ten. UFN places describe powers of successive primes, starting with two at the right, then three, then five. The teaching route calls these primes unique factors. In familiar arithmetic, ○, ◠, and ◡ read as zero, one, and negative one.

UFN makes multiplicative structure visible and lets calculations preserve it. For whole numbers, divisibility compares entries; greatest common factors take the smaller entries, and least common multiples the larger. Addition can use shared factors, but finding the factors of an arbitrary sum can still be hard.

Where unique spelling ends

Finite products of primes raised to rational powers have unique factor coordinates. This includes positive fractions and products of roots. Clear the exponent denominators, then apply the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.

Arbitrary exponents can describe the same value in different ways: 3 = 2log₂ 3. General sums and limits need not have a factor spelling. The language can keep them as exact recipes.

Square and curly lists give operations a consistent grammar. Counters, limits, and direction rules add mathematical structure around the factor representation. The lessons below build these ideas from steps and journeys.

A written journey

Every mark tells how far to go from wherever you are. Put journeys inside square brackets to follow them together. Walk the first, then the next. The result tells where you finish compared with where you began.

A complete written journey is called an expression. Its value is the change from its start to its finish. The route can be longer than that change. These journey values are numbers. The mark = says “ends at the same place,” so we can compare different expressions.

Read one layer at a time

When an expression looks busy, read it in three passes:

First, find the outer brackets. Next, find their complete entries: the pieces directly inside them. Keep each inner bracketed expression together. Finally, read what the outer brackets ask you to do. Square brackets ask you to join the journeys.

The outside brackets tell you what to do. Work out an inner entry before doing that job. Cover the rest of the line or draw a box around the entry you are reading.

Undo a whole journey

A bar inside square brackets says to undo everything written after it. Undoing a forward journey takes you back; undoing a backward journey takes you forward. We call joining journeys addition; the result is their sum. Undoing a journey is called subtraction.

In [|…], nothing comes before the bar. Start at ○ and take the opposite of the journey after it.

Use [|…] to reverse a whole journey. The mark ◡ is itself a backward step; it is not a prefix to put before another number.

What happens when each step changes size?

Make each step twice as long

Take a three-step journey. Now make each step as long as a two-step journey. You reach as far as six of your original steps.

Write this as {[◠ ◠ ◠] [◠ ◠]}. The first entry gives the journey. The second tells what each of its forward steps becomes; each backward step changes in the opposite way. Here we can also reach the same place by joining two copies of the first journey.

Changing a journey's size is called scaling. For now, our journeys stay on the original path. Later we will give them other directions and use curly brackets to turn them too.

[□ : ◠ … [◠ ◠] : [◠ ◠ ◠]] = {[◠ ◠ ◠] [◠ ◠]}{[◠ ◠] [◠ ◠]} = [◠ ◠ ◠ ◠]

Keep a step, reverse it, or remove it

If each forward step becomes ◠, the journey stays as it was. If it becomes ◡, the journey reverses. If it becomes ○, every step makes no change of place. Reversing a backward step makes it go forward.

For any curly list, begin with ◠ and apply its entries from left to right. With no entries, you still have ◠. An entry ○ does give an instruction: each step becomes no change of place, so the whole journey finishes at ○.

What happens when you change how fast a sound repeats?

Start with a repeating sound

A steady musical sound comes from a repeating vibration. Its frequency tells how often that vibration repeats. Faster repetition gives a higher pitch.

Keep one sound as your starting point. A change of ⟨◠⟩ makes another repeat twice as fast. Musicians call this distance an octave. A change of ⟨◠◡⟩ asks for three repetitions in the time the starting sound makes two. In Western music theory, this distance is called a fifth.

The name comes from counting positions in a scale: a chosen sequence of notes from lower to higher pitch. In the familiar do–re–mi scale, count from do to sol: do, re, mi, fa, sol. That is five positions, including both ends. The three-halves ratio gives the exact tuning of this distance.

The number describes a change in repetition rate. It does not describe loudness or how long the sound lasts.

    Every share uses the complete input with a forward count joined to it. Each power uses that same complete input as its exponent. An input with several components is handled as one journey.

    Rules, names, and definitions
    • ○ ◠ ◡ describe staying, stepping forward, and stepping backward.
    • [] joins journeys; {} combines changes. Their bars ask for undoing.
    • ⟨⟩ holds factor instructions. Lower corners choose a starting place; * asks for a unique factor’s position.
    • Upper corners choose an exponent or ask which exponent reaches a target.
    • @ puts an amount along a direction; # reads a component.
    • Ranges name visits and use their values in recipes. () keeps the entries as a sequence.
    • Lower corners after an endless range choose how to measure convergence.

    ≔() declares a replacement rule. Digit, polynomial, and Fourier forms need their declarations; you can edit those meanings.

    ┌┘ and ⟳ have defining recipes, but the calculator supplies their fixed names and recognizes them directly. Using a name does not prove the limit of its defining recipe.

    Read the complete rule summary ↗

    Words to keep apart
    Expression / value
    The written instructions / what they describe. Different expressions can have the same value.
    Amount / size
    An amount can be forward or backward along a path. Size is distance from the start under the chosen measurement, without a direction.
    Entry / place
    An entry is a complete piece of a list. A factor position chooses its unique factor; a sequence place counts entries to its right.
    Name / visited value
    A counter’s name stays fixed. Its range supplies a new value on each visit.

    Rules that every rewriting must keep
    • Keep inner expressions together as complete entries.
    • Apply curly entries and visit sequences in their written order. Reorder changes only when a rule permits it.
    • Direction labels choose paths without scaling amounts.
    • Expansion preserves the names used by supplied expressions.
    • Every required input must have a value. {○ {|○}} is undefined.
    • A finite preview is not an endless recipe’s limit.
    • An unfinished calculation keeps its exact recipe; a decimal picture supplies no exact proof.
    • Font shapes change the drawing of the marks. A shorter equivalent result is a different written spelling of the same value.

    Find an exponent

    BASE⌈|X⌉ asks which exponent makes BASE reach X. BASE must be forward of ○ on @○ and differ from ◠. A forward target on that path gives its path logarithm; a target with a nonzero part on another path uses the principal logarithm. Backward targets on @○ require a branch choice. Zero is undefined.

    BASE⌈E|X⌉ first raises BASE to E, then uses that result as the logarithm's base. It means BASE⌈E⌉⌈|X⌉. Each side of the bar holds one expression. See worked examples.

    Directions

    A@○ puts amount A along the original path. A@◠, A@⟨◠⟩, and A@⟨◠○⟩ use the other three directions. A must itself be a value on the original path. Curly products preserve their written order.

    Read a component

    #○, #◠, #⟨◠⟩, and #⟨◠○⟩ read the forward or backward amount along the selected axis. The answer lies on @○. An absent contribution gives ○. These labels name axes, not angles.

    @ and # apply from left to right. Upper brackets bind first: ⟨◠⟩@◠#◠⌈⟨◠⟩⌉ raises the label. Use [⟨◠⟩@◠#◠]⌈⟨◠⟩⌉ to raise the reading instead. See component examples.

    Find a position

    *⟨◠○⟩ asks for the place of ⟨◠○⟩ in the list of unique factors. Its answer is ⟨◠⟩. A position attachment can use that result directly. The input must be a unique factor.

    The star includes lower corners attached to its input. Upper corners, @, and # apply to the answer. Use square brackets to look up a longer expression: *[⟨◠⟩ ◠] gives ⟨◠⟩.
